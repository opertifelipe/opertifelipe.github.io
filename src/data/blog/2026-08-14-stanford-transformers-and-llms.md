# A Stanford lecture worth your time

I recently watched the first lecture of [Stanford CME295: Transformers & LLMs — Autumn 2025](https://youtu.be/Ub3GoFaUcds), and I strongly recommend it to anyone interested in understanding—or revisiting—how Transformers and Large Language Models actually work.

There is no shortage of material about LLMs. Much of it, however, focuses on frameworks, APIs and the latest model release. Those resources can be useful, but they often move quickly past the ideas that make these systems possible. This Stanford lecture does the opposite: it takes the time to rebuild the conceptual path toward the Transformer.

## A particularly effective progression

The lecture begins with a broad view of Natural Language Processing and then moves through tokenization, word representations and recurrent neural networks. From there, it introduces self-attention and develops the Transformer architecture before closing with a detailed example.

That progression is one of its greatest strengths. The Transformer is not presented as an isolated diagram to memorise. Instead, the lecture shows why earlier approaches were useful, where their limitations emerged and why attention represented such an important change.

This makes the material valuable even if you already work with LLMs. Concepts that have gradually become familiar vocabulary—embeddings, attention, queries, keys, values and contextual representations—recover their precise meaning when they are placed back into the full architecture.

## More than a beginner's introduction

I would describe this lecture as both accessible and technically meaningful. It can serve as an introduction for someone building a serious foundation in modern NLP, but it is equally useful as a structured review for data scientists, machine-learning engineers and AI architects.

For experienced practitioners, revisiting the fundamentals is never wasted time. We often interact with LLMs through increasingly high-level abstractions, and it becomes easy to think primarily in terms of prompts, agents or APIs. Returning to the mechanics of representation and attention helps reconnect those abstractions to the model underneath them.

The section on self-attention is especially useful in this respect. Attention is sometimes described almost as magic; seeing it developed as a concrete mechanism for relating tokens and constructing context makes the behaviour of modern language models much easier to reason about.

## Why I recommend it

The lecture is clear, rigorous and well paced. It does not simplify the subject to the point of losing substance, and it does not assume that naming the components is the same as explaining them. The detailed example at the end is particularly effective because it turns the architecture from a collection of blocks into a process that can be followed.

My recommendation is to watch it actively: keep notes, pause during the architectural explanations and try to reconstruct the flow of information yourself. Even if you are already familiar with Transformers, you will probably notice details that had become blurred through daily use.

The full lecture lasts around one hour and forty minutes, and it is absolutely worth the time. For anyone who wants a high-quality refresher on Transformers—or a stronger understanding of how LLMs work in general—this is one of the most interesting resources I have encountered recently.

**Highly recommended:** [watch Stanford CME295 Lecture 1 on YouTube](https://youtu.be/Ub3GoFaUcds).
